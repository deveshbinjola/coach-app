How Anthropic's own engineers actually prompt

Four people who build and shape Claude sat down for an hour on prompt engineering. Almost none of it is about clever phrasings. It is about clear thinking, honest instructions, and reading what comes back. The fourteen signals worth keeping.

Alex Albert · DevRel David Hershey · Applied AI Amanda Askell · Finetuning Zack Witten · Prompt Engineer
Source: "AI prompt engineering: A deep dive" (Anthropic) · ~56 min · 15,074 words
The fourteen signals

Each grounded in what they actually said.

01

It is clear communication, not magic words

The whole game is stripping the fog out of your instructions. Fancy prompt formulas are mostly noise.

"Talking to a model is a lot like talking to a person. Getting in there and understanding the psychology of the model."Zack Witten
02

The "engineering" is the iteration loop

The real unlock is the restart button. You revert to a clean slate and try again, cleanly, hundreds of times. The skill is not one perfect prompt. It is the willingness to hammer and watch what breaks.

"In a 15-minute span I'll be sending hundreds of prompts to the model. Back and forth, back and forth."Amanda Askell
03

Read the outputs, obsessively

The ML cliché is "look at your data." The prompting version is "look at the model's outputs." Read how it got there, not just pass or fail. People write "think step by step" and never check the model is actually doing it.

"There's just fundamentally so much to learn between the lines of what it's writing and why and how. It's not just did it get the task right."David Hershey
04

Externalize your brain

The hardest part is untangling everything you know that the model does not, and writing it down. Bad prompts are over-conditioned on the author's private context. This was named the best summary of prompting in the whole talk.

"Take any person off the street who's reasonable, and just externalize your brain into them. That's the core of prompting."Amanda Askell
05

The temp-agency test

Picture a competent contractor who knows your industry but just walked in and does not know your company. That is the model. Brief it the way you would brief that person. Stop hunting for the one perfect insightful line.

"They're pretty competent, they know a lot about your industry, but they don't know the name of your company. What would you say to that person?"Zack Witten
06

Role-prompting is mostly dead

"You are a high-school teacher" makes the model do a similar task, not your task, and you lose the nuance. Be prescriptive about the real context instead ("you are the support chat window in this product"). And be honest. The tipping and lying tricks are not needed on modern models.

"As models are more capable and understand more about the world, I just don't see it as necessary to lie to them."Amanda Askell
07

Give the model an "out"

For weird inputs, tell it to return unsure in tags instead of forcing an answer. Bonus: reviewing the "unsures" surfaces all your broken test cases and cleans your data for free.

"If something weird happens and you're really not sure what to do, just output in tags: unsure. Give it something to do."Amanda Askell
08

Test the ugly edges, not the happy path

Empty string. A dataset with zero matching rows. A photo of a goat where a chart should be. And remember real user traffic: no shift key, typos every other word, no punctuation. Your polished eval is not your traffic.

"They never used the shift key and every other word is a typo. They think it's Google."David Hershey
09

Give it the paper. Respect the model

People baby Claude and dumb things down. Instead, hand it the source material and let it work at full capacity.

"People will start describing the technique, and I'm like, give it the paper. Here's a paper, write down 17 examples of this."Amanda Askell
10

Make the model debug your prompt

Two moves that work. First: "Do not follow these instructions, just tell me what's ambiguous." Second, after a mistake: "You got this wrong. Rewrite my instructions so you would not." Often it fixes itself.

"Can you maybe write an edited version of my instructions that would make you not get it wrong? A lot of the time, the model just gets it right."Amanda Askell
11

Research and production prompts diverge in the last mile

Research wants diversity, so use few, deliberately un-representative examples (teach with a children's story a task you will run on legal docs) so it does not latch on. Production wants reliability, so pile on examples and cover the whole input space, because it runs ten million times.

"I'll take examples that are very distinct from the ones I'm running on. I care more about you understanding the actual thing I want."Amanda Askell
12

Chain-of-thought does real work

Not just "space to compute." Swap correct reasoning for plausible-but-wrong reasoning and the answer flips. Telling it to output "um, ah" filler tokens does not help. The reasoning is load-bearing.

"Having it write a story before it finished a task did not work as well. Clearly the actual reasoning part is doing something toward the outcome."Alex Albert & Zack Witten
13

Know when to quit

If the model clearly cannot do it, do not grind for months. Hershey burned a weekend getting Claude to read a Pokémon screen from "no signal" to "some signal," then walked away. Waiting for a better model beats a perfect prompt for an impossible task.

"I could grind on this for four months, and the thing that would come out is another model. I'm just gonna wait for another model."David Hershey
14

The future is elicitation, and it flips

Today you interview the model. Soon it interviews you, because it will know the domain better than you do. Prompting becomes introspection: making yourself legible to the model, not teaching a slower student. Hershey already does this.

"I've started having Claude interview me a lot more. The hardest thing is pulling the right information out of my brain, then turning that into a prompt."David Hershey

What to actually do with this

The five moves worth stealing this week.

  • Brief it like a temp, not a role. Give the real context and the real task. Drop "you are a [job]."
  • Read ten outputs before you touch the prompt. The fix is almost always visible in how it reasoned.
  • Have it interview you. "Ask me questions until you have everything you need to do this," then turn the answers into the prompt.
  • Make it flag its own confusion. "Tell me what's ambiguous before you answer." Then patch those gaps.
  • Give it an escape hatch. An "unsure" tag turns edge cases into a cleanup queue instead of silent wrong answers.
